# A.R.S. § 13-2929 — state harboring and transporting offense

## Excerpt

> It is unlawful for a person who is in violation of a criminal offense to
> transport or move an alien in this state, or to conceal, harbor, or shield
> an alien from detection, knowing or in reckless disregard of the fact that
> the alien has come to, entered, or remains in the United States in
> violation of law.

## Formalization notes — caught twice, on independent grounds

This provision is now reported in **two** findings, which mirrors how the
Ninth Circuit actually decided it:

1. **Field preemption.** [`us-ina-harboring`](us-ina-harboring.md) carries
   `exclusive: true`, so this rule is displaced for existing in an occupied
   field — matching the opinion's section headed "§ 13-2929 is Field
   Preempted."
2. **Contradiction.** 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(C)'s religious safe harbor
   (formalized in
   [`us-ina-harboring-religious-exemption`](us-ina-harboring-religious-exemption.md))
   permits what this statute forbids, since Arizona enacted no equivalent
   carve-out — matching the opinion's section C on conflict preemption.

Both were once misses. The second was invisible until categorical conditions
let the exemption be formalized; the first until `exclusive` existed. Neither
was fixed by rewording the corpus.

### Why this rule keeps the bare harboring key

Georgia's and South Carolina's harboring offenses are keyed to *narrower*
activities, because each requires an extra element, and so neither produces a
safe-harbor contradiction. This statute has a superficially similar predicate
("a person who is in violation of a criminal offense") yet keeps the bare
key. The reason is that the Ninth Circuit held that very element **void for
vagueness** — it was unintelligible — so it does not function to narrow the
offense, and the same panel expressly found the safe-harbor conflict real.
Treating a vague element as a narrowing one would discard a conflict a court
actually found. See [`ga-hb87-7`](ga-hb87-7.md) for the contrast.

## Status

**Preliminarily enjoined — not finally struck down.** *Valle del Sol v.
Whiting*, 732 F.3d 1006 (9th Cir. 2013) (No. 12-17152, filed Oct. 8, 2013)
affirmed the district court's *preliminary* injunction. The panel held
§ 13-2929 void for vagueness under the Due Process Clause (because the
element "in violation of a criminal offense" was unintelligible) and, **in
the alternative**, preempted — holding it **field** preempted (section B)
and **conflict** preempted (section C) in consecutive sections. Judge Bea
concurred as to vagueness but dissented from reaching preemption at all.

This provision was not among the four at issue in *Arizona v. United
States*, which is why it was litigated separately.
