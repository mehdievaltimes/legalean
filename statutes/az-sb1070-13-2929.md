# A.R.S. § 13-2929 — state harboring and transporting offense

## Excerpt

> It is unlawful for a person who is in violation of a criminal offense to
> transport or move an alien in this state, or to conceal, harbor, or shield
> an alien from detection, knowing or in reckless disregard of the fact that
> the alien has come to, entered, or remains in the United States in
> violation of law.

## Formalization notes — a false negative, and not the kind you'd guess

Both this rule and the federal one are `prohibited`, so the tool finds no
contradiction and reports nothing. But the reason it misses a real conflict
here is **formalization fidelity**, not a missing preemption doctrine.

The Ninth Circuit's conflict-preemption reasoning turned partly on the fact
that 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(C) contains an express **safe harbor for certain
religious activities**, and this Arizona statute contains no equivalent — so
Arizona would punish conduct Congress deliberately protected. That is
exactly the allowed-vs-prohibited shape this tool *can* detect. It is missed
only because [`us-ina-harboring`](us-ina-harboring.md) is formalized as a
flat `prohibited`, dropping the exemption. Capturing it would need a
condition like `religious_volunteer == true`, which the single-predicate
condition model can express but `candidates.py` deliberately excludes
(categorical conditions produce no integer witness).

The lesson: the tool is only ever as good as the hand-formalization, and the
interesting conflicts often live in the exceptions.

## Status

**Preliminarily enjoined — not finally struck down.** *Valle del Sol v.
Whiting*, 732 F.3d 1006 (9th Cir. 2013) (No. 12-17152, filed Oct. 8, 2013)
affirmed the district court's *preliminary* injunction. The panel held
§ 13-2929 void for vagueness under the Due Process Clause (because the
element "in violation of a criminal offense" was unintelligible) and, **in
the alternative**, preempted — resting expressly on **conflict**
preemption. Judge Bea concurred as to vagueness but dissented from reaching
preemption at all.

This provision was not among the four at issue in *Arizona v. United
States*, which is why it was litigated separately.
